# How the Saba National Marine Park Protects Our Reefs

The Saba National Marine Park protects the island's reefs through a practical combination of legal zoning, permanent moorings, fishing and anchoring restrictions, visitor fees, diver education, and consistent local management. Established in 1987, it was created to preserve the quality of an extraordinary marine resource before it was damaged, not to repair it afterward.

That preventative idea remains one of the most important parts of diving in Saba.

## A park around the whole island

The park circles Saba from the high-water mark to a depth of 60 meters or 200 feet, including the seabed and the water above it. It also encompasses the offshore seamounts and protected areas that help define the island's diving.

This matters because conservation here is not limited to one showcase reef. It is built around the island as a connected system.

## Zoning and moorings

A zoning plan divides the park for different recreational and commercial uses. Permanent mooring buoys allow dive boats to secure without dropping anchors onto coral and other sensitive bottom habitat.

Moorings may seem like a small operational detail. They are not. Repeated anchoring in the same popular places can cause cumulative damage. A maintained buoy system is one of the simplest and most visible ways protection becomes part of every dive.

## Rules divers experience directly

Saba's diving guidance is deliberately practical:

- Do not touch coral or harass wildlife.
- Do not remove anything from the marine park.
- Do not feed fish or leave anything behind.
- Practice excellent buoyancy control.
- Follow the dive leader's plan.
- Do not use gloves except where permitted for holding the line at a safety stop.
- Photographers should prepare camera settings before framing and must not contact the reef.
- Light sticks are not permitted.

These are not vague environmental slogans. They are behaviors that reduce cumulative damage across thousands of dives.

## Fishing, anchoring, and diver education

The Saba Conservation Foundation connects the park's fish abundance to restrictions on fishing and anchoring and to extensive diver education. Healthy reef systems need both rules and everyday compliance. Dive professionals reinforce those expectations during briefings, buoyancy guidance, and wildlife interactions.

## Visitor fees and self-sustaining management

The park raises revenue through visitor fees, souvenir sales, and donations. It is widely described as one of the few self-sustaining marine parks in the world.

Sea Saba currently lists a $3 per-diver-per-dive Saba Marine Park contribution and a separate $1 per-diver-per-dive hyperbaric chamber contribution. These are externally set charges, not simply operator profit, and they connect each visitor directly to conservation and emergency infrastructure.

## Why protection matters to divers

Protection is visible underwater: large sponges, complex coral structure, turtles, groupers, sharks, rays, macro life, and reef systems that still feel alive. No marine park can guarantee encounters or eliminate every threat, but long-term protection improves the chance that the reef divers enjoy today will remain worth visiting tomorrow.

## How visitors can help

- Choose licensed dive operators.
- Maintain neutral buoyancy before using a camera.
- Never touch, feed, chase, or collect marine life.
- Use moorings and follow local boating rules.
- Pay required nature and park fees willingly.
- Support the Saba Conservation Foundation or Sea & Learn where appropriate.
- Treat conservation rules as part of good diving, not inconvenience.

## The simple answer

The Saba National Marine Park works because protection is built into daily use. Zoning, moorings, rules, fees, education, and local management all reinforce each other. Divers benefit from healthier reefs, and their behavior helps determine whether that success continues.
