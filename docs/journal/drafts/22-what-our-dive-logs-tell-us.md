# What Our Dive Logs Tell Us About Saba

Sea Saba's dive logs are not scientific surveys, but they are a valuable record of where we actually dive, what conditions guests experience, and which animals are recorded often enough to shape real expectations. A read-only analysis of 3,351 deduplicated public dive records from September 24, 2023 through November 25, 2026 reveals warm water year-round, a broad spread of sites, and frequent recorded turtle and shark encounters.

The numbers are most useful when their limits are stated clearly.

## What the dataset is

The analysis used the public Sea Saba Dive Log data already rendered on the website. Multiple records describing the same physical dive are grouped by the site's existing public-data logic. Species counts represent animals recorded during a dive, not unique individuals. Blank sightings do not necessarily mean no animals were present, and changing logging habits can affect comparisons.

## Water temperature

Valid water-temperature readings show a narrow tropical range. Monthly means were about 80°F in February and March and about 85°F in October, with most months between those points. In practical terms, Saba is warm-water diving year-round, though repetitive dives, depth, wind, and personal cold tolerance still matter.

## Where dives happen

The most frequently recorded named sites in the extract were Hot Springs, Tent Shallow, Babylon, Customs House, Diamond Rock, Tent Reef, Third Encounter, and Man O'War Shoals. That distribution reflects both site quality and operational reality: conditions, guest mix, training, access, and scheduling all influence frequency.

A frequently visited site is not automatically better than a rarely visited one. Some compelling sites are conditional, advanced, seasonal, or less compatible with the average recreational group.

## Turtles and sharks

At least one turtle was recorded on 65.9% of dives in the extract. At least one shark was recorded on 68.3%. Nurse sharks appeared in 47.7% of records and Caribbean reef sharks in 42.4%.

Those figures should be read as operational sighting records, not scientific encounter rates. They do not count unique animals, establish abundance trends, or guarantee future encounters. They do, however, support honest language that turtles and sharks are commonly logged in the diving experience.

Some sites show strikingly different patterns. Shallow and mixed-structure sites such as Diamond Rock, Man O'War Shoals, Customs House, Hot Springs, and 50/50 had high proportions of dives with turtle records. Deep pinnacle sites such as Outer Limits, Twilight Zone, and Third Encounter had far fewer recorded turtle encounters but can offer other kinds of dramatic marine life.

## Seasons and limitations

Both turtle and shark records appeared across the year. That does not mean every month is identical or that there are no biological patterns. Recreational dive logs are not designed to answer those questions. Weather, site choice, observer effort, and inconsistent recording can all create apparent patterns.

Scientific monitoring, such as the shark research conducted around Saba and the Saba Bank, uses more controlled methods. Dive logs complement that work; they do not replace it.

## Why the records still matter

The logs make diving visible. They show the range of sites used, the rhythm of operation, changing conditions, and the kinds of encounters guests actually record. They also create accountability: if we say turtles or sharks are common, we should be transparent about what kind of evidence supports that statement.

## The simple answer

Three years of operational records suggest warm water, frequent turtle and shark sightings, and a diverse but condition-driven site portfolio. They are evidence of lived diving experience, not population science.
