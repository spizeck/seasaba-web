# Sharks of Saba: What Divers Can Expect to See

Divers around Saba may encounter nurse sharks, Caribbean reef sharks, and occasionally other shark species, especially around deeper structure, sandy ledges, walls, and volcanic pinnacles. Sharks are a real and important part of the island's marine ecosystem, but no site, season, or species encounter can be guaranteed.

That honest answer is part of what makes shark diving meaningful. These are wild animals moving through a protected but dynamic ocean environment.

## Which sharks are most associated with Saba?

### Nurse sharks

Nurse sharks are bottom-oriented sharks often seen resting beneath ledges or cruising slowly across sand and reef structure. They have barbels near the mouth and are generally calm around divers when left undisturbed.

In Sea Saba's public dive-log extract, Atlantic Nurse Shark was the most frequently recorded shark category. That does not mean every dive produced a nurse shark. It means they were commonly logged across many recreational dives.

### Caribbean reef sharks

Caribbean reef sharks are active, streamlined predators often associated with deeper reef edges, walls, pinnacles, and blue-water structure. Seeing one pass cleanly through the water column is a signature Caribbean moment for many divers.

Scientific monitoring summarized by the Dutch Caribbean Nature Alliance also identifies nurse sharks and Caribbean reef sharks as common species in research around Saba and the Saba Bank. That research is separate from recreational dive sightings and should not be confused with it.

### Blacktip reef sharks and other species

Blacktip reef sharks appear in the dive-log species catalog, and research in the wider Dutch Caribbean has documented additional reef-associated shark species. Treat occasional identifications carefully. Lighting, distance, behavior, and diver experience all affect species recognition, and not every shark-shaped silhouette can be identified confidently.

## What Sea Saba's dive logs show

Sea Saba's public dive-log export analyzed for this draft covered 3,351 deduplicated recreational dive records from September 24, 2023 through November 25, 2026. Across those records, shark sightings were recorded at least once on 68.3% of dives. Atlantic Nurse Shark sightings appeared on 47.7% and Caribbean Reef Shark sightings on 42.4%.

Those numbers need careful interpretation:

- They are recorded sightings, not unique animals.
- The same shark can be counted on multiple dives or by multiple groups.
- Some dives may have incomplete or missing sighting entries.
- Records come from recreational dive sites chosen for guest experience, not randomized scientific transects.
- They cannot show population trends or prove that a species is increasing or decreasing.

Within those limits, the records still show something valuable: sharks are not a rare novelty in Sea Saba's logged diving experience.

## Where divers often see sharks

The extracted records show different patterns across sites. Ladder Bay-area sites such as 50/50, Babylon, and Ladder Bay Drift had high proportions of dives with recorded shark sightings. Third Encounter also showed a high proportion, consistent with its deeper structure and open-water setting.

Perhaps surprisingly, Shark Shoals did not produce a universal sighting record. In the extracted data, shark sightings were recorded on 56.2% of 48 Shark Shoals dives. That is still substantial, but it demonstrates why even a shark-named site cannot be sold as a guarantee.

Site choice, current, season, time of day, diver behavior, and chance all matter.

## Why sharks matter to the reef

Sharks are part of balanced marine ecosystems. As predators, they influence prey behavior and help remove vulnerable or injured animals. Their presence is one indicator of a functioning reef system, although sightings alone are not enough to judge ecosystem health.

Saba's marine park protections, including restrictions on fishing and anchoring and a long-established mooring system, help create conditions where sharks and other large species can remain part of the diving experience.

## How to behave around sharks

Good shark encounters are calm encounters.

- Do not chase, corner, touch, or feed sharks.
- Maintain controlled buoyancy and normal breathing.
- Keep the animal's escape path open.
- Let the shark choose the distance.
- Follow the guide's positioning and instructions.
- Avoid crowding a resting nurse shark.
- If a shark leaves, let it leave.

Flash photography is less important than behavior. A calm diver who stays still often gets a better natural view than a diver who pursues the animal.

## Are shark dives dangerous?

Sharks are wild predators and deserve respect, but the presence of a shark does not automatically mean danger. Most shark encounters in recreational diving are uneventful when divers remain calm and do not harass or feed the animal.

Risk cannot be reduced to zero, and species behavior varies. The correct approach is informed respect, not fear or complacency.

## The simple answer

Expect the possibility, not the promise. Nurse sharks and Caribbean reef sharks are part of Saba's diving environment, and Sea Saba's operational records show that shark sightings are frequently logged. The best encounters happen when divers choose appropriate sites, follow the guide, and allow wild animals to behave naturally.
